Decision one

Buying your web address

A domain is the address people type. You rent it yearly. It is separate from where the site is built, which is already handled and costs nothing.

Buy it in your own name, with your own credit card, on your own account. This is the one piece of the whole setup you should never let anyone else own — if you and a designer ever part ways, the domain is what you cannot rebuild.

Recommended

Buy it through Netlify — $13.99 the first year, $16.99 to renew

Netlify is the service already running your website, so buying the address there means it is connected the moment you pay for it. Nothing to point anywhere, nothing to copy between two different companies, no settings to get wrong. It costs a few dollars more than the cheapest option and saves an entire step that is easy to fumble.

It also handles the security padlock in your browser bar automatically, which is otherwise a thing someone has to set up.

Decision two

The scheduling calendar

Your site has two calendar spaces already built into it — one for private sessions, one for the monthly group. You sign up for a booking service, send Richard the link, and both go live.

Everything you described is standard: private one-on-ones, plus a recurring group event that stops taking bookings at ten people. You do not need anything unusual or expensive.

Look at this one first

Cal.com

Free for one person

  • Unlimited session types on the free plan, so private and group are separate
  • Seated events with a seat count — your ten-person cap
  • Attendees can be hidden from each other in a seated event
  • Takes payment through Stripe or PayPal at booking
  • Open source, so the whole thing can be moved elsewhere later

Confirm on their pricing page that seated events and the embed are on the free plan before committing — Cal.com moves features between tiers fairly often.

Calendly Standard

$12 / month

  • The most familiar to clients — most people have booked through it before
  • Group events with a seat limit
  • Payment at booking through PayPal

The safest, dullest choice. Its free plan only allows one session type and no payments, so it will not cover what you need.

Acuity Scheduling

from $16 / month

  • Proper "class" bookings with a seat count
  • Intake forms before the first session
  • Session packages and prepaid bundles

More capable, more to learn. Worth moving to if you later want intake forms or want to sell blocks of sessions.

Why Cal.com is worth the look, specifically for your group. In a seated event it can hide attendees from one another, so ten survivors can book the same session without any of them seeing the others' names or email addresses on a confirmation. For a group of abuse survivors that is not a nice-to-have — a leaked attendee list is a real safety problem, and most booking tools show everyone to everyone by default.

A note on "open source" and hosting

Cal.com is open source, and you may see that it can be self-hosted for free. That is true, but it does not mean it runs on the same hosting as your website. Your site is a set of files served straight to visitors — there is nothing running behind it. A booking system needs a live server and a database, running all the time, to hold appointments and send reminders.

So there are two honest options: use Cal.com's own hosted version (free for one person, and what I would do), or pay for a separate server to run your own copy — roughly $10 to $20 a month, plus setup, plus somebody keeping it patched. Self-hosting is worth it when you have a technical team. You do not need that, and the hosted free plan is the same software.

One thing to set up on day one, whichever you choose: a buffer between appointments. Fifteen minutes after each session, automatically. This work is heavy, and back-to-back bookings will wear you down faster than anything else on this list.

Also worth setting: how far ahead people can book, and a cutoff so nobody books you for two hours from now.

Decision three

Taking payment

The payment page is built and waiting for three pieces of information. Send Richard these and it goes live.

  • VenmoYour username, and confirmation you want it shown publicly.
  • PayPalYour PayPal.me link — set one up free at paypal.me if you do not have one.
  • ZelleThe email address or phone number people should send to. Zelle works through your own bank's app; there is nothing to sign up for.

Set up a business profile on Venmo and PayPal rather than using your personal one. It keeps your client payments separate from your grocery split-ups, which your accountant will thank you for, and it means clients are not looking at your personal transaction feed.

Once your LLC exists: open a business checking account and point all three payment methods at it. Mixing business income into a personal account is the single most common thing that undermines the liability protection an LLC is supposed to give you.

How it works

Changing your own website

You have an editor at julienneblackburn.netlify.app/keystatic. It looks like a form. You fill it in, press Save, and the website updates itself a minute later. No code, and nothing you can break.

  1. 1

    Sign in

    Go to julienneblackburn.netlify.app/keystatic and sign in. Bookmark that page; it is the only address you need. When your own domain is connected, the editor moves with it — same page, shorter address.

  2. 2

    Pick what you are changing

    Down the left you will see Prayers, Videos, Sunshine, Gallery, Testimonies, and then the page content — Home, About, Coaching, Schedule, Payment, Contact.

  3. 3

    Write, then Save

    Every field is labeled in plain language. Writing a prayer works like writing an email — type it, bold what you want bold, save it.

  4. 4

    Wait about a minute

    The site rebuilds itself after each save. Refresh the page and your change is there.

Nothing is ever really deleted. Every save is kept as its own version, so anything you change can be put back exactly as it was. Write freely — you cannot wreck it.

Every prayer, video, and practice also has a Draft checkbox. Tick it and the piece stays hidden while you work on it, then untick it when you are ready.

The review gate

Art and testimonies reach you first

You asked to pray over each submission before it goes up. That is built in as a hard rule, not a habit you have to remember.

  1. 1

    Someone submits

    They fill in the form on your site, upload a photo of their piece, choose how they want to be credited, and tick a box giving permission. Nothing is published by this step.

  2. 2

    You get an email

    The submission and the image land in your inbox and in a list only you can see.

  3. 3

    You review and pray over it

    Take as long as you want. Nothing is on a clock.

  4. 4

    Only then does it go up

    In the editor you add the piece and tick "Reviewed, prayed over, and approved to publish." Until that box is ticked, the website behaves as if the piece does not exist. If you decline something, you simply never add it.

Testimonies work exactly the same way, with the same approval box.
